## The fare decides more than the map does

Seat selection is sold, not given. The cheapest fare on almost any airline
assigns a seat at check-in and charges to move it; the fares above it open the
map earlier and take the fee away.

So the first useful question is not "which seat" but "what did the fare buy".
If choosing costs money, the honest comparison is that fee against the fare one
step up, which often includes the seat and a bag as well.

{.callout}
> **On a flight under two hours, this matters much less than it feels like it
> does.** Take an aisle if you expect to get up, a window if you expect to
> sleep, and spend the decision somewhere else.

## Rows with something particular about them

Exit rows have more legroom and, usually, a seat that does not recline. They
also carry conditions: you have to be able to help in an evacuation, which
rules out anyone travelling with an infant or needing assistance themselves.

The row directly in front of a galley or lavatory is the one to avoid if you
are hoping to sleep — it is the busiest few square metres on the aircraft for
the whole flight, and the seat in front of a bulkhead often does not recline
either.

Bulkhead rows have no seat in front, which means legroom without a reclining
headrest in your face, and no under-seat storage for take-off and landing.

## Window, middle, aisle

Aisle is for getting up: the lavatory, a stretch, an early exit. The cost is
being the one who stands when the two people beside you want out, and a
shoulder in the traffic.

Window is for sleeping: something to lean on, control of the shade, and nobody
climbing over you. The cost is asking twice on a long flight.

Middle is the one nobody picks, which is why it is often the last seat with a
fee attached and the first to be assigned free.

## What a seat map will not tell you

Two seats with the same number on the same route can be different seats, because
the aircraft can be different. A schedule change, an equipment swap the night
before, or a route that alternates between two types will all quietly move you.

The map also does not show the things people most regret: a misaligned window,
a seat beside a galley curtain, a recline blocked by the row behind being an
exit. Those live in seat-review sites, which are worth ten minutes on a long
flight and nothing at all on a short one[^1].

[^1]: Seat layouts change with cabin refits, so a review of a specific seat is
only as current as the refit it was written after.
