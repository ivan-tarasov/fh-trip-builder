## Three separate permissions

Flying with an animal means satisfying three parties who do not talk to each other: the airline, which decides whether the animal can be carried and how; the destination country, which decides whether it may enter; and, on a connection, every country in between.

They can disagree. An airline that will carry a cat in the cabin does not thereby get it into a country that requires a rabies titre test months in advance, and a country that will admit a dog says nothing about whether the aircraft has a hold that is pressurised and heated for it.

{.callout}
> **Start with the destination, not the airline.** Import rules take the longest to satisfy and are the only ones you cannot negotiate.

## In the cabin, in the hold, or as cargo

In the cabin is the easiest and the most limited: a small animal in a carrier that fits under the seat in front, with a hard limit on combined weight that is usually somewhere around eight kilograms, and a limit on how many animals the cabin takes on any one flight. That last one is why it is booked early.

In the hold means a checked animal in a container meeting live-animal standards. It is how most dogs travel, and it is the option most affected by temperature: many airlines suspend it in summer and winter on particular routes, because the risk is on the tarmac rather than in the air.

As cargo is a separate booking on a separate document, and it is what larger animals and some breeds are restricted to regardless of size.

## Breed restrictions are a real constraint

Short-nosed breeds — bulldogs, pugs, Persian cats and their relatives — are refused in the hold by many airlines and restricted by others. The reason is that they breathe less efficiently under heat and stress, and both are worse in a hold than in a living room.

This is not a formality to be argued around at the desk. It is a written policy, it is checked, and an animal refused at the airport is a trip that ends there.

## The paperwork, roughly

Microchip first, then rabies vaccination — in that order, because a vaccination recorded before a chip usually does not count. Then whatever the destination adds: a titre test with a waiting period, a health certificate issued within a few days of travel, an import permit, a treatment for tapeworm administered in a specific window before arrival.

The waiting periods are what catch people. A titre test can require months between the blood draw and the flight, which makes this a decision taken before the trip is booked rather than after[^1].

[^1]: Requirements differ by country and change; a government import page is the only source worth trusting on a specific route, and it is worth re-reading close to departure.
